WHAT TO WRITE
  ONE LINE FOR EVERY LINE OF THE LYRICS ABOVE. If the song has 44
  lines, write 44. Not 16, not "about that many" - exactly as many, in
  exactly the same order.

  This is the most important rule here. Your line 12 is what is on
  screen while lyric line 12 is being sung, and the tool lines them up
  one to one. Get the count wrong and it cannot: an earlier video
  played "Meow, meow, meow!" over a duckling and "Quack, quack, quack!"
  over a puppy, because there were sixteen scenes for forty-four lines
  and the tool had to share them out by the clock.

  EACH LINE MUST SHOW WHAT ITS LYRIC SAYS. Read the lyric, then write
  the picture of it. If the lyric names a character or the sound one of
  them makes, THAT character is the subject of the shot and it should
  usually be "Close up of the ..." with them doing the thing the line
  says - not the main character doing something while they happen to be
  in the background.

  ONE CHARACTER PER SHOT. One. Either the main character on their own,
  or ONE of the others on their own - never two of them in the same
  line.

  This is not a style preference, it is what the picture model can
  actually draw. Asked for a boy and a kitten it merges them: one
  drawing came back as the boy with a cat's ears on his head and no
  kitten anywhere, another as a duck with a dog's face. Two subjects
  in one frame is the thing it is worst at, and there is no wording
  that fixes it - so do not ask.

  It is also how the big children's channels cut: single subjects,
  intercut. A shot of the puppy, then a shot of the boy, reads better
  than both of them squeezed into one frame.

  One shot per line. Nothing else.

  Each line must contain, in this order:
    1. ONE physical action the main character does. One. Not two joined
       with "and then". It must be something a camera could film - "he
       claps his hands", not "he feels happy".

       It has to happen WHERE THEY ALREADY ARE. Never write that they
       walk in, walk over, run to, come towards, step up to or walk
       away. A model told to walk someone into the shot walks them out
       of it again, and the second half of the clip is an empty field.

       Make it big, and make it SLOW. Each shot is four seconds long
       and the action has to fill them without hurrying. "He claps his
       hands twice" and "he waves one hand slowly above his head" are
       right. "He claps five times" and anything with "quickly" or
       "rapidly" in it comes back frantic.

       Never more than two of anything. Never the words "quickly",
       "rapidly" or "fast".
    2. NOTHING. There is nobody else in the shot - see the rule above.
    3. One small piece of background life - and BEGIN THAT CLAUSE WITH
       THE BACKGROUND ITSELF: "flowers nodding", "butterflies drifting
       past", "leaves swaying", "clouds drifting slowly", "grass
       rippling gently". The tool tells a background clause from a
       character clause by its first word, so "the wind moves the
       flowers" is read as a character and kept when it should be
       dropped.
    4. The words "the camera does not move" - unless that shot genuinely
       needs a slow push in, in which case write "the camera pushes in
       slowly".

  Start about a third of the lines with "Close up of" or "Wide shot of"
  so the video is not one framing over and over - but "Close up of" is
  for the OTHER characters only. A close up of a small child comes back
  with the top of their head cut off. For them, use "Wide shot of" or
  nothing at all.

  Write in the present tense.

  Keep each line between 18 and 28 words.

RULES
  - Plain text only. No numbering, no bullets, no quotes, no bold, no
    headings, no blank lines between them, no commentary before or
    after. Just the lines - one for each line of the lyrics, and the
    same number of them.
  - No text, words, letters or lyrics appearing in the picture.
  - Nobody enters or leaves a shot. Whoever is in it is there at the
    start of the line and still there at the end. They may bark, hop on
    the spot, flap, sit, wag - not run over or waddle in.
  - Whoever is in a shot must be DOING something, described. An earlier
    video left the puppy sitting like an ornament because the line only
    said it was there. "the puppy in the grass" is not enough; "the
    puppy in the grass wagging its tail" is.
  - COUNT YOUR LINES BEFORE YOU SEND. It must be exactly the number of
    lyric lines. An earlier attempt came back one short, and one short
    means every line after the gap is on the wrong lyric.
  - Nothing violent, nothing frightening, nothing sad.
  - The shots should follow one another as a little story.
  - Give each of the other characters at least one close up of its own.
